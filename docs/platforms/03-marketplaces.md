## 1. The licence question comes first

Do not read past this section until you can answer it. Every channel below will ask for licence documents before they ask for anything else. If the answer is not ready, the rest of this pack is a plan you cannot action.

### The shape of the system, in plain terms

South Africa splits liquor regulation across two levels, and they do different jobs.

**National.** The National Liquor Authority, sitting inside the dtic, registers the manufacturing and distribution tiers. A distribution registration lets you sell liquor to another registered or licensed business. It does not let you sell to the public. This is the registration that matters when a bottle store, a bar, a restaurant or an online retailer wants to buy stock from you.

**Provincial.** Each province licenses retail selling, on-consumption and off-consumption, under its own act. For you that is Gauteng. There is no separate "online" licence in South African law. Selling to a member of the public over the internet is retail, and it sits under an off-consumption licence held by whoever is doing the selling.

**SARS.** Separate from both, spirits carry excise duty and manufacturing spirits involves a customs and excise licence. Every retailer that onboards you will assume your stock is excise-compliant. Confirm the position with SARS Excise and your accountant, and establish in writing who carries it, you or whoever produces the liquid.

### What this means for your two routes

**Direct to consumer, your own site.** You are the retailer. That is a provincial off-consumption question, in Gauteng, and it lives with whoever holds the licence at the Silverton premises. If verboten.co.za is already taking orders and delivering, someone has answered this. Make sure you can produce the certificate on demand, and make sure the trading name and address on it match what you put on supplier forms.

**Through third parties.** They are the retailer. They sell under their licence. You are a supplier selling business to business, which is the national distribution tier, not the provincial retail tier. This is a genuinely different registration from the one that lets you ship a bottle to a customer in Durban. Many small producers discover this only when a buyer asks for the certificate.

The first internal question, before anything external: **who actually holds the manufacturing position for Verboten?** If the liquid is produced under contract by a licensed distillery, the manufacturing licence sits with them, and what you personally need may be only the distribution registration plus your existing retail position. If you manufacture in Silverton yourselves, that is a different and larger set of paperwork. This changes the whole answer and I cannot see it from here. It also changes what section 3 is allowed to say about where the brandy is made, so settle it early.

### Where my knowledge stops

I am not a lawyer, I have not seen your licence file, and liquor licensing in South Africa varies by province, changes, and has been actively argued over since 2020, particularly around online sales and delivery. I am not going to invent thresholds, timelines or fee amounts. What follows is the general structure, which is well established. The detail is not mine to give.

## 2. The landscape, ranked for a premium brandy with three SKUs

### The money maths that gates all of it

Before you talk to any buyer, know what you can afford to give away. At a 15% VAT rate, R450 including VAT is R391.30 excluding VAT. That is the number a retailer works from. Confirm the current rate with your accountant before you quote off this table.

| Retailer margin on their ex VAT selling price | Your ex VAT price to them | Your price per bottle including VAT to them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20% | R313.04 | R359.99 |
| 25% | R293.48 | R337.50 |
| 30% | R273.91 | R315.00 |
| 35% | R254.35 | R292.50 |
| 40% | R234.78 | R270.00 |

These are the bands you will be negotiated into. I cannot tell you your cost of goods, so I cannot tell you which of these rows is survivable. Work that out on a page before your first buyer call, because the number they ask for is the number you will be stuck with across the whole channel.

Two rules that will save you later:

**Price parity.** Whatever a retailer sells at, it should be R450. If a channel discounts below your own site, your own site becomes the expensive place to buy your own brandy. Put a recommended retail price in writing in every quote.

**Payment terms.** Independents usually pay on delivery or within about a month. National chains work on longer terms counted from statement, which for a young brand is a working capital problem, not a sales win. VERIFY the terms per account, in writing, before you ship.

### Tier 1: Independent bottle stores and premium on-trade. Start here.

**What it is.** Owner-run liquor stores and the bars and restaurants that care what is on the back shelf. Your own canon already names this: quality bars that know their stuff, restaurants that care what they serve.

**What it demands.** A distribution position that lets you invoice a licensed business. A trade price. Samples. A one-page sell sheet. You delivering the stock yourself. That is close to the whole list.

**Fees.** Usually none. Occasionally a bar wants a menu listing contribution or staff training in exchange for a pour. Consignment is available and worth offering: you leave six bottles, you keep ownership, they pay for what sells. It removes every reason for a nervous owner to say no.

**Suits you?** Yes. Highest margin, smallest commitment, fastest to start, and it is where a brandy brand is actually discovered. One person with limited time can call on ten stores and ten bars in Pretoria and Johannesburg in two weeks.

**Where to start.** Walk in. Ask for the owner or the buyer, in person, mid-morning on a weekday, with a bottle in hand. This channel does not respond to email cold. Use the email in section 5 only as a follow-up after the visit.

### Tier 2: Specialist online liquor retailers. The right shelf.

**What they are.** Online-first liquor retailers with real premium spirits ranges, holding their own licence, taking your stock as a supplier and selling to the public under their own name and their own age gate. These are the closest match to a R450 South African brandy that is not yet a household name.

I have not confirmed that any of these currently take on new suppliers or list third party brands. Look at each range first, then ask. In roughly the order I would go:

- **Norman Goodfellows**, ngf.co.za. Long-standing wine and spirits retailer with physical stores and an online shop. A listing there is a reference other buyers recognise.
- **Deeliver**, deeliver.co.za. Online liquor retailer. Check the range and the delivery footprint on the site before you write.
- **The Grand Store**, grandstore.co.za. Online liquor retailer with a spirits range.
- **Mothercity Liquor**, mothercityliquor.co.za. Online liquor retailer, Cape Town based.
- **Liquor.co.za**. Online liquor retailer. Worth a look at their range mix before you write.

**What they demand.** VERIFY per retailer. In general expect: your licence and company documents, a trade price with a margin in the bands above, product data and images to their spec, a minimum first order, and stock held by you until they order. Some ask for a marketing or listing contribution, a feature slot fee, or a discount funded by you for a launch promotion. None of these are standard, all of them are negotiable, and I am not going to quote numbers I have not seen.

**Suits you?** Yes, second. Low volume commitment, correct customer, and their site does the age gating and the payment for you.

**How to check the real commercials.** Do not guess and do not rely on this pack. Go to each site, find the contact or trade page, and email the buyer asking for their new supplier pack and listing requirements. Section 5 has the email. Ask three questions in writing: margin required, payment terms, and any listing or marketing fees.

### Tier 3: Takealot. Big reach, real complications.

**What it is.** The largest general online retailer in South Africa, running both its own retail buying and a third-party marketplace for independent sellers.

**The honest picture on liquor.** Takealot's alcohol category has a history. Press reports in December 2023 and January 2024 said it halted alcohol sales over liquor licence complications relating to e-commerce, and that the category ran with non-alcoholic mixers only for a period afterwards. Today the public liquor category page presents itself as selling liquor and spirits. What I could **not** confirm from public pages is the thing that actually matters to you: whether a third-party marketplace seller may list alcohol at all, or whether liquor is bought and held by Takealot as retail stock only. Those are two completely different businesses for you, and only Takealot can tell you which one is open.

**What it demands, if you are eligible.** I am not going to quote a fee schedule I cannot see, and neither should any blog you read. The structure sellers are charged on has several parts stacked on each other: an account or subscription fee, a success fee set as a percentage of the selling price and varying by category, a per-unit fulfilment fee driven by size and weight, plus storage and return handling. A 750ml glass bottle is heavy and breakable, which pushes the fulfilment and damage side of that stack up. Check current terms and the live fee schedule at seller.takealot.com, then model it against the margin table above with real numbers before you commit anything.

**Suits you?** Not yet. Restricted category, uncertain eligibility, a heavy fee stack on a fragile heavy item, and it puts you in a grid next to every mass-market brandy sorted by price. The one SKU where it could make sense later is the can, and only in a multipack.

**Exactly what to check, in this order.** Register at seller.takealot.com or takealot.com/sell, then ask Seller Support in writing: (1) can a marketplace seller list alcoholic products, (2) if yes, what licence documentation is required from the seller, (3) what is the success fee for the brandy and spirits category, (4) what are the fulfilment fees for a 750ml glass bottle. Get it in email. Do not take a forum answer.

### Tier 4: National chain liquor retail. Later, if at all.

Makro, Ultra Liquors, Checkers LiquorShop, Pick n Pay Liquor, Tops at Spar, Liquor City and similar.

**What they demand.** Formal supplier onboarding, national or regional volume, guaranteed supply continuity, promotional grid participation part-funded by you, extended payment terms, and in many cases fees or rebates attached to listing and to promotion. VERIFY the specifics with each group's supplier team, because they differ significantly and none of them publish it.

**Suits you?** Not now. A three-SKU brand with no marketing budget gets listed, gets no shelf support, does not rotate, and gets delisted in two cycles, having funded the promotion that failed. That outcome costs you money and a reference.

**The one exception worth knowing.** Some groups are decentralised. Spar in particular is built on independently owned stores served by regional distribution centres, and regional buyers sometimes take local products for local stores. If you want one national-group conversation, make it a Gauteng regional one, not a head-office one. VERIFY the current process by phoning the regional distribution centre for your area and asking who handles new local liquor suppliers.

### Tier 5: Quick commerce apps. Not a channel you can sell into.

Checkers Sixty60, Pick n Pay asap and Bottles, Zulzi, Uber Eats, Mr D. You do not have a supplier relationship with these. They sell the stock of the retailer behind them. You reach them only by getting listed in Tier 4 first. Set them aside entirely for now.

### Tier 6: Gifting and corporate. Small, seasonal, decent margin.

Gift hamper companies, corporate gifting agents, and premium subscription boxes buy in short bursts around Father's Day, Heritage Day and the December season. Volumes are small, margins are usually better than retail, and the two-bottle set is built for exactly this. Worth a batch of emails in September and October, not before. Anything aimed at a corporate gifting list still carries the 18+ line, and the buyer needs to confirm recipients are over 18.

## 3. Finished product data, ready to paste

Two things apply to every listing below, without exception. Each one carries the line "Drink responsibly. Not for sale to persons under 18." And the retailer must run its own age gate at checkout. If a channel cannot age gate, do not list there.

### On listing titles, and the trade-off

Marketplace search is not brand voice. A shopper types "brandy 750ml" or "south african brandy gift". The title field is the strongest search signal on almost every platform, and it is also the line that appears in a grid of forty competing bottles. Retailers commonly reject titles that carry slogans, promotional language or repeated keywords. VERIFY each platform's title rules in their own seller guide.

Your voice is quiet, spare and dark. That is a problem only if you assume a title has to sell. It does not. The compromise is this: **the title may be plain, but it must never be loud.** Brand, product, key attribute, size, strength. No superlatives, no "best", no "award-winning", no exclamation, no tagline, no keyword stuffing. Plain is not a betrayal of restraint. Shouting would be.

The story goes in the description, where you have room and where the reader has already chosen to look. That is the correct division of labour.

### Barcodes: you must supply these

Almost every retailer beyond your own site requires a unique GTIN, in practice an EAN-13 barcode, for each individually sellable SKU. Nobody can generate these for you and free ones off the internet cause real problems later when two products in a retailer's system share a code.

- You need one barcode per sellable unit: the 750ml bottle, the two-bottle set, the single can, and each can multipack you decide to create.
- Cases shipped to a distribution centre often need a separate outer case code as well. VERIFY per retailer.
- The issuing body in South Africa is GS1 South Africa, gs1za.org. Go there, not to a barcode reseller.
- Budget time as well as money. Do this in the first week, because it blocks every listing below.

## 4. Image requirements

### The specs marketplaces typically demand

These are the requirements that come up again and again across South African marketplaces and online retailers. Build to the strictest version and you cover most of them. VERIFY the current numbers in each platform's own image guide before you shoot, because they do change and I am working from what is commonly stated, not from a current spec sheet in front of me.

| Requirement | Typical spec |
| --- | --- |
| Background on the primary image | Solid pure white, RGB 255 255 255, no transparency, no gradient, no shadow spill |
| Dimensions | Minimums vary, commonly stated around 600 pixels square, with a maximum in the low thousands. Shoot at 2000 x 2000 or larger so zoom works and every platform is covered |
| Aspect ratio | Square, 1:1, strongly preferred |
| Product fill | Product occupies most of the frame, commonly stated as 85% or more |
| File type and colour | JPEG or PNG, sRGB colour. Pixel dimensions are what matter, DPI is not a meaningful setting for web images |
| File size | Caps vary by platform. Keep files under 2MB where you can and you will not hit one |
| Not allowed on the primary | Watermarks, logos placed over the image, text overlays, badges, borders, frames, props, backgrounds, models, multiple products, packaging that is not being sold |
| Secondary images | Lifestyle, detail and context shots are usually allowed here. This is where your existing work belongs |

### Why this breaks your current assets

Your studio shots are on black. That is the right look for verboten.co.za, for Instagram and for a sell sheet. It is the wrong file for a marketplace primary image, for two reasons.

First, most platforms will simply reject it, because pure white backgrounds are a hard requirement, not a preference.

Second, even where it is accepted, marketplace grids are white. A black tile in a white grid does not read as premium, it reads as a broken image or a badly cropped photo. The restraint that works on your own dark page works against you on someone else's white page.

This is not a crop or a retouch job. The lighting on a bottle photographed for black is built around dark reflections in the glass, and those reflections look like dirt when the background is cut to white. **You need new shots.** Say that out loud before somebody spends a week trying to mask the existing ones.

### The shot list you actually need

Shoot all three SKUs in one session.

**Primary, white background, square, one per SKU**

1. Verboten Premium Brandy, 750ml, front label straight on, bottle upright and centred
2. Verboten Premium Set, both bottles, arranged as they are sold
3. Verboten Brandy & Cola can, front facing

**Secondary, white background**

4. Back label, sharp enough to read the legal text. Buyers and compliance teams both ask for this
5. Angled three-quarter view of the bottle
6. Closure and neck detail
7. The set with its outer packaging, if it has one
8. A scale reference shot if the platform allows one

**Secondary, brand style**

9. Your existing black studio shots, used in the additional image slots where lifestyle is permitted
10. Two or three of the real event photographs, where allowed, showing the bottle in a genuine setting

### How to get it done without a budget

A large sheet of white paper or card curved from a table up a wall, one window as the light source, a white board opposite the window to bounce light back, a phone or camera on a tripod, and the product a good distance in front of the background so the light falls off cleanly. Shoot slightly wide, crop to square, then clean the background to pure white. If you would rather buy the time back, a half day with a product photographer who shoots for e-commerce will produce all of the above, and you should brief them with this section and the words "marketplace compliant, pure white, 2000 pixels square".

Two compliance points that catch people.

Do not put an 18+ badge, a responsibility line or any legal text as an overlay on the primary image. Text overlays are a rejection reason. The warning belongs in the description and on the label itself.

If a person appears in any secondary or event image you use commercially, they must be over 25 and must clearly look it, and you need their permission to use the shot. Nothing showing a vehicle, a driver, a sports setting, a workplace, or anyone who could be read as under age. Check the event photographs against that before you hand any of them to a retailer.

## 6. The honest verdict

You have a new brand, effectively zero traffic, no analytics, one person with limited time, no white-background photography, and at least one product showing out of stock. In that position, the temptation is to chase the biggest channel. That is the wrong move, because the biggest channel demands the most preparation and rewards the least differentiated thing about you.

Here is what to do, in order.

### Weeks 1 and 2: unblock yourself

1. Answer the licence question. One paid hour with a Gauteng liquor licensing attorney will tell you exactly what you hold and what you need to supply third parties. Nothing below can start without it.
2. Buy barcodes from GS1 South Africa for all sellable SKUs. This blocks every listing and takes real time.
3. Fix the Brandy & Cola stock status on your own site, or hide the product.
4. Build the document folder from section 1.
5. Decide your trade price using the margin table in section 2, and write it down as a one-page trade price list with a minimum order quantity and a recommended retail price.

### Weeks 3 and 4: get shootable and sellable

6. Shoot the white-background image set from section 4. All three SKUs, one session.
7. Take the sell sheet already written in section 5, fill in the bracketed fields, add the new bottle shot and export it as a PDF. Every conversation in month 2 needs it.
8. Install analytics on verboten.co.za. You are about to send people there and you currently cannot see what happens.

### Month 2: sell where selling is cheap

9. Walk into fifteen independent bottle stores and premium bars across Pretoria and Johannesburg with samples and the sell sheet. Offer consignment to the nervous ones. This is the highest return use of your time in this whole pack, and it costs almost nothing.
10. Email the four or five specialist online liquor retailers in Tier 2. Ask the three questions. Take the best answer.

### Month 3 and beyond, only if months 1 and 2 worked

11. Ask Takealot Seller Support, in writing, whether marketplace sellers may list alcohol and what the fees are. Model it honestly against the real fee schedule. Only proceed if the can in a multipack pays for itself.
12. Approach gifting and corporate buyers in September for the December season, leading with the two-bottle set.
13. One regional conversation with a decentralised group like Spar, in Gauteng only.

### What to leave alone

- **National chain liquor listings.** They cost more than they return for a brand at your stage, and a failed listing is harder to recover from than no listing.
- **Quick commerce apps.** Not available to you directly. Set them aside.
- **Paid advertising on Meta or TikTok.** Alcohol advertising is restricted on both, requires age targeting, and paid spend on a brand with no proven organic pull is money you can spend on stock or photography instead. Post organically behind age gates and keep the cash.
- **A gin conversation with any buyer.** It has no launch date. Mentioning it makes the brandy sound like a stepping stone.

### The one-line version

Sort the licence and the barcodes, shoot on white, then sell the brandy face to face in Gauteng and to two or three specialist online retailers. Everything else on this list is a distraction until the first bottle sells through somebody else's till.
